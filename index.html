HWK 4.1 — Spoken Time

Context of use

Who: Knowledge workers who glance at a smartwatch dozens of times a day — usually not because they need the numeric value of the time, but because they need a verbal answer: "it's almost half past ten, I should wrap up." Where: Smartwatch face or ambient desktop widget. Problem solved: Every clock — analog or digital — shows time as a number that the brain then silently translates into words before acting on it ("10:28" → "almost half past, meeting soon"). Spoken Time removes that translation step: it renders time in the words you would actually say aloud. Why choose this over any existing smartwatch face? No stock watch face does this. Apple, Garmin, and Pixel faces all present numerals or hands and leave the verbal conversion to the user; Spoken Time delivers the pre-verbalized answer, and lets the wearer tune how precise that answer is (EXACT / CASUAL / VAGUE) — matching how humans naturally vary time precision by social context. The conversational rounding ("ALMOST HALF PAST TEN") and the adjustable precision are value that no off-the-shelf face provides. This matters to me personally because I caught myself reading "10:13" on my watch and telling a friend "quarter past" — the watch made me do the rounding it should have done for me.

Sketches I explored (1–5: smartwatch face designs)

Why I chose Sketch 5 (Word Clock)

I originally prototyped Sketch 3 (big-character digital), but instructor feedback was blunt and fair: a large HH:MM with date and weather is a replication of what every smartwatch already ships with — the rationale reduces to "why people own smartwatches." So I returned to my sketch set and picked the one design that questions an assumption instead of restyling it: Sketch 5, time written in words ("IT IS HALF PAST TEN"). It re-encodes time from numerals into natural language — the representation people actually use when they speak. Against the alternatives: the binary clock (Sketch 2) is novel but fails the glanceability requirement of the smartwatch context, the minimal analog (Sketch 4) is exactly the "conventional reproduction" the brief warns against, and the IoT rings (Sketch 1) replicate Apple's activity face. The word clock is the only sketch that is both novel and faithful to the glance-context.

Design decisions in the final clock

1. Words, not numerals — encoding choice. Time is mapped to natural-language phrases instead of digits. This is a deliberate re-encoding of the data: the same hour()/minute() values, but represented in the reader's output vocabulary rather than the clock's input format. The phrase hierarchy (dim "IT IS" → bright bold time words → dim daypart) uses size and value contrast so the time words are read first.

2. Conversational rounding. In CASUAL mode the clock rounds to the nearest five minutes and prepends "ALMOST" or "JUST AFTER" — e.g., 10:28 renders as "ALMOST HALF PAST TEN". The modifier is set in the accent color because it carries the freshest information (which side of the landmark you are on), just as a speaker stresses it in conversation.

3. Click to change precision (EXACT / CASUAL / VAGUE). The core interaction. EXACT spells the full time ("TEN TWENTY-EIGHT"), CASUAL speaks like a colleague ("ALMOST HALF PAST TEN"), VAGUE answers like a friend on a weekend ("MID-MORNING"). Humans choose time precision by social context; no existing watch face exposes precision as a user control. This is the feature that makes the design something a stock smartwatch cannot do.

4. Progress arc = time until the words change. The cyan arc completes exactly when the phrase will flip (per minute in EXACT, per 5-minute bucket in CASUAL, per hour in VAGUE). Motion is therefore mapped to the display's own data refresh, not to a decorative seconds hand — the arc answers "how stale is what I'm reading?"

5. Day/night background gradient. Kept from the earlier prototype: the face interpolates between four anchor colors (deep navy at midnight, cool blue at dawn, neutral at noon, warm purple at dusk), a redundant low-attention encoding of the daypart that the words state explicitly.

Iteration log (instructor + peer feedback)

  1. Iteration 1 (big-character prototype): Built a large HH:MM face with date and weather rows, day/night gradient, and click-to-cycle layouts (see commit history).
  2. Iteration 2 (instructor feedback): "Design is not unique — why would your audience choose this over any current smartwatch interface?" I could not answer it, which meant the concept, not the execution, was wrong. Pivoted to Sketch 5 and rebuilt the face around natural-language time with conversational rounding.
  3. Iteration 3 (peer feedback): A classmate testing the word clock said "half past ten" felt wrong at 10:28 — "I'd say almost half past." Added the ALMOST / JUST AFTER modifiers so the rounding direction is explicit, and gave the modifier the accent color.
  4. Iteration 4 (peer feedback): Another reader asked "when would I ever need the exact minute, then?" — which surfaced that precision is contextual, not fixed. Added the click-to-cycle EXACT / CASUAL / VAGUE modes and re-mapped the progress arc to time-until-phrase-change per mode.
  5. Iteration 5 (self-testing): Found awkward phrases at edge times — "JUST PAST FIVE PAST FIVE" at 5:07 and "TWELVE O'CLOCK IN THE AFTERNOON" at noon. Changed the modifier to "JUST AFTER" and added an "AT MIDDAY" daypart for the 12 o'clock hour.

Self-reflection / future work

The English phrase grammar is hand-built; supporting other languages (Chinese "十点半" or the Norwegian "halv elleve" convention, which counts toward the next hour) would test whether the encoding generalizes — different cultures round time differently. I would also like the precision to adapt automatically: calendar-aware context (5 minutes before a meeting → EXACT; Saturday morning → VAGUE) instead of a manual click. Finally, a fade transition when the phrase flips would make the change itself glanceable rather than instantaneous.

HWK 4.2 — Workday Progress Bar (Final Prototype)

Context of use

Who: Remote and hybrid workers tracking how much of the workday is left. Especially people in flow-state work who lose track of time. Where: Desktop browser tab, second-monitor widget, or pinned in a productivity dashboard. Problem solved: "How much of the workday is left" is something people compute in their head every hour. A loading-bar metaphor turns that question into a familiar UI affordance instead of a math problem.

Sketches I explored (6–10: office worker tools)

Why I chose Sketch 8 (Progress Bar)

All five sketches in this group address office time-tracking, but the progress bar hits a sweet spot: zero learning curve (everyone has watched a software loading bar), precise (percentage is unambiguous), and dense enough to support secondary information (time blocks, remaining time, current time) without becoming cluttered. The coffee cup and stamp card are charming but lower-resolution; the subway map is interesting but more about milestones than continuous progress; the email inbox skews task-list rather than time-aware.

Design decisions in the final clock

1. Bar = whole workday. The bar spans 9 AM to 6 PM and fills as the day progresses. The fill width directly maps to elapsed workday — no math required.

2. Big-percentage primary readout. A 96pt percentage sits above the bar so users get the answer at peripheral glance. The bar itself supplies the spatial context.

3. Color shifts with the time of day. Progress fill, active block card, and the REMAINING countdown all share an accent color that interpolates cool-blue → teal → amber → warm-red across the workday. The "temperature" of the bar tells you intuitively where you are, even without reading the number.

4. Three-block decomposition. Below the bar I show MORNING / AFTERNOON / EVENING cards with the active period highlighted. This adds a coarser mental model — "I'm in afternoon" — to the precise percentage.

5. Click to cycle workday presets. Clicking the bar swaps between STANDARD (9–6), EARLY (8–5), and LATE (10–7) shifts. Same visual language, different schedule. This prevents the clock from being useful only to one cohort.

Iteration log (peer feedback + self-testing)

  1. Iteration 1 (self-testing): The first version showed only the progress bar without a percentage label. I found I was eyeballing the fill width to estimate progress. Added a 96pt percentage above the bar — primary readout in one glance.
  2. Iteration 2 (peer feedback): A classmate noted "I see the bar but I don't know what time it actually is." Added the bottom info card with CURRENT TIME (HH:MM:SS) and REMAINING (Xh XXm) — making the abstract progress concrete.
  3. Iteration 3 (self-testing): A flat blue bar felt "the same" all day. Replaced the fixed color with a workday-temperature gradient (blue → teal → amber → red). An afternoon screenshot now reads warmer than a morning one — the color reinforces the percentage.
  4. Iteration 4 (peer feedback): Reviewer asked "what if I work a different shift?" Added the click-to-cycle workday presets. Same UI, three schedules, with a badge above the bar showing the active preset.

Self-reflection / future work

Persist the chosen preset to localStorage. A drag handle to set custom start/end times. Deeper accessibility — currently the design relies on color for the workday-temperature cue, so a colorblind-safe alternative encoding (pattern or icon) would be the next addition.
